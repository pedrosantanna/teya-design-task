# Illustrations

Getting Started, the splash and large icons use small 3D objects modelled in code from boxes, cylinders, spheres and capsules. They are drawn like a technical illustration, with palette colours in two flat tones, one line weight for every edge and a flat cast shadow, and a viewer on this page shows each one from any camera.

## Style rules

| Rule | In practice |
| --- | --- |
| Simple forms | A few large boxes, cylinders, spheres, capsules and extruded profiles. Few small parts and almost no rounded bevels, so each object still reads at 64 to 120 px |
| Palette colours only | Fills come from the [colour](colour.md) ramps: pale steps for large surfaces, 500 to 900 for small accents |
| Two tones per surface | Lit faces show the palette colour. Faces turned from the light show the colour × 0.56 × an ambient tint, mixed 36% toward the background's dark tone. The ambient tint is the background's hue; the dark tone is the 800 step of its ramp (900 on Indigo 800), near-black on Biscuit and indigo 900 on the dark grounds |
| Screen-space outlines | After the scene is drawn, one pass compares each pixel's object, surface direction and depth with its neighbours. A line appears at a silhouette, where two parts meet, and where two faces of one part meet at 35° or more. Lines are near-black #1a1714 and a fixed width on screen (see Line weight); MSAA and FXAA keep curves smooth |
| Parts sit on surfaces | A part touches the surface it belongs to instead of sinking into it, and no two visible faces share a plane, so nothing flickers where they meet |
| Solid shadow | One light, from the upper left unless moved in the viewer. The shadow is a flat shape on the ground in the background's own shade tone; on an object with its own ground, such as the street corner, it darkens that ground by the same rule |
| Isometric view | Objects are shown at the true isometric angle (35.264° elevation, 45° turn); the viewer also offers free orthographic and perspective cameras |
| Motion | On entry, parts drop in order and land with a small squash. A part resting on another squashes with it, so nothing sinks into the part below; ground slabs drop without squashing. Idle motion is slight. With Reduce Motion, the object appears complete and stays still |

Two references shaped these rules. Gooch, Gooch, Shirley and Cohen ("A non-photorealistic lighting model for automatic technical illustration", SIGGRAPH 1998) shade by shifting hue instead of darkening toward black, and outline silhouettes and creases; here the background gives the hue. Saito and Takahashi ("Comprehensible rendering of 3-D shapes", SIGGRAPH 1990) find outlines from discontinuities in depth and normal images of the scene; the outline pass does the same, with an object id added. Colouring shadows from the scene's palette instead of greying them is also common in flat illustration and in games such as Monument Valley.

## Getting started

Each Getting started page shows its object on that page's ground: the crossroads on Indigo (page 1), the shop on Peach (page 2) and the delivery box on Pistachio (page 3). Pages 2 and 3 draw theirs at 312 pt (1.3 times the earlier 240); page 1 is a hero that bleeds off the screen (below). The objects are rendered for their own ground, so shade and shadow mix toward that colour's 900 step (Peach 900, Pistachio 900; near-black on the dark Indigo page), and on a transparent ground: the page colour blends while the shopper drags, and an opaque square would show its edge mid-swipe. When the grounds were all Indigo, the shop's Indigo 800 parts had to be recoloured to Peach 500 on dark page 2; on the Peach dark tile its own Indigo roof stands apart, so the recolour is gone. Each object's bounding box, without its cast shadow, is centred in its image (`fit` in the manifest: `center` 0.8 of the frame, with the object shrinking until the whole shadow sits 3 pt inside it), and the image is centred on the screen, so the object is centred on the screen whatever way its shadow falls; before, the shop and the box were centred with their shadow and sat to the left. Stills ship at @2x and @3x, the entry clips of the shop and the box at @2x, and the shop also has its idle loop. Every page plays its clip (entry, then idle where there is one, then the still) when it is shown and shows the still with Reduce Motion.

### Page 1 hero

The High Street crossroads on page 1 is drawn about 2.2 times the screen width (874 pt on a 402 pt screen) and the screen crops it at the sides, so the detail of the four blocks shows: shop fronts, awnings, the phone box and pillar box, the people at the crossings. The export renders a window onto the scene, 460 × 420 pt (the `bleed` size), instead of the whole object: `fitCenter` 1.9 sets the scene at 1.9 times the window's width, and `focusX` and `focusY` move the window's centre across the scene by a fraction of its width and height (`fitFrame` turns this on). I set it on the corner with the phone box, the pillar box and the café awning, with the low block in front cut by the bottom edge. The window has 29 pt to spare on each side of a 402 pt screen, which the slow drift uses (the art moves 12 pt each way over 9 s). On a wider screen, such as the web preview, the art is scaled up so that it still bleeds.

The line is 1.4 pt at the size it is displayed, the same as every other illustration, because the window is rendered at 1 pt per pt. The ground is transparent. The window's lower edge dissolves into the ground over its last 150 pt (`fadeBottom`, applied when the frames are encoded). The earlier 40 pt ramp read as a band, and 70 pt turned the pale building faces lavender over the Indigo. The ramp now follows the way Apple fades art into a ground: it is long, it is smootherstep (6t⁵ − 15t⁴ + 10t³, whose slope and curvature are both 0 at the start and at the end, so there is no point where the eye finds the beginning or the end), and the picture blurs progressively as it thins. Four levels of Gaussian blur, from none at the start of the ramp to 5 pt at the bottom edge, are cross-faded row by row, computed on premultiplied colour so the transparent ground does not darken the edges. The alpha stays under the colour, so the art keeps blending into the page colour while the shopper drags. In dark mode the encoder multiplies the colour by 0.9 (`dim`), so the cream city sits calmer on Indigo 800. The ground the art is shaded for in dark mode is Indigo 800, the page's token.

It has an entry clip: the four blocks drop in one after another, which the page plays when it is shown, and then the last frame holds with the slow drift on top. As an animated WebP with alpha the clip was 1.4 MB for each of light and dark, even at half the frame rate, which put the set at 6.4 MB against the 5 MB budget. It is an MP4 instead (H.264, CRF 23, `video: true` in the manifest), baked on the page's ground like the clips on the other flat grounds, and it takes 0.8 MB in all. A video has no alpha, so while the pages move (the scroll is between two pages) the screen shows the transparent still in its place; the two are the same pose and the same fade. There is no idle loop for the crossroads, since the walkers would add as much again. The still (@2x, @3x) takes 0.5 MB in all. With Reduce Motion the art shows at rest and does not drift.

### Why the art did not play

On the web and on the device every Getting started clip started and was handed over at once. The generated manifest stored each clip's length (`ms`) from the render's frame folders, which are gitignored, and wrote 0 for every clip whose folder was not on the machine that ran the encoder. The component ends an entry after `ms` and switches to the idle loop or the still, so a length of 0 meant the entry was cut off in the same frame it began (the shop went straight to its idle loop, the box to its still). The encoder now reads the length from the file itself, the sum of an animated WebP's frame delays or an MP4's duration, whenever no frame folder is there to say.

### Location ask

The location ask's screen is the Background in both modes. `color.location` is the ground the art is rendered for: the Background in light mode, Stone 360 in dark, where the art sits on a soft radial lift in that colour (about 1.9 times the art's size, fading to nothing) instead of a screen of its own, which read as another app. The quiet street (a lamp column and a bench) is rendered at 352 pt (1.6 times the earlier 220 pt), framed so that the pair's bounding box, without the shadows, is centred in the image, and the image is centred between the status bar and the headline, with the iron recoloured from Stone 320 to Stone 740 in dark mode. In the scene the lamp column stands behind and to the left and the bench lower, in front and to the right, turned a quarter so that its length runs from the bottom left to the top right against the diagonal of the pair, as in Two objects below; the bench moved back from in front of the column, so the whole column shows. The earlier dark render drew the near-black iron on the Background and the outlines vanished into it. I measured the dominant fills and the outline ink (#1A1714) against the candidates:

## Category cards

Each category card's art is a transparent image, 120 × 164 pt, shaded for that kind's tile colour (read from the colour tokens, `shop.accent.<hue>.tile`) and drawn over the tile in the app. The object's bounding box, without its shadow, is centred in the top 120 pt square and its longer side is 80% of that square, so every object has the same footprint. The shadow falls where the light sends it, and the object shrinks until the whole shadow sits inside the square with at least 8 pt to spare on every side (`fit.clear`), so no card edge cuts it; the bakery and the café were cut on the right before. The Category screen's header shows the same square. The earlier round placed the object with its shadow in mind, which left it small (about 22% of the square) and high.

Each object is checked on its contact sheet for the face a shopper reads first. The isometric camera already shows the most readable face of 18 of the 19 objects. The film camera showed its lens at a slant under its top plate, so its set turns the camera 35° (`rot` in the manifest) and the lens faces the viewer; the light turns with it. A turn of 25° the other way hid the lens and was dropped.

### Two objects

When a card shows two objects, the taller one stands behind and to the left and the lower one lies in front and to the right, so the pair reads along one diagonal, from the top left to the bottom right: the chemist's bottle and box, the deli's jar and wedge, the pencils and the notebook, the bag of baguettes and the loaf, the kettle and the mug, the milk and the paper, the hammer and the tin of nails. A long object (the notebook, the hammer, the newspaper, the loaf, the bag's wide face) turns against that diagonal, its length running from the bottom left to the top right, so the two directions cross. They may overlap a little. A single object is not bound by this.

The encoder replaces the corner-pixel check with two others: the top-left pixel of every category still must be fully transparent, and the tile each still was shaded for (`categories.grounds.json`) must equal the token's current value, since a changed token leaves the shadow tone off.

## Line weight

The outline pass works in device pixels. Its width is `line` pt at the display size times the output scale, minus 0.75 px that FXAA and edge anti-aliasing add, and the pass covers a pixel by how far the nearest edge is, so the width is fractional (1.4 pt gives 2.05 px at @2x and 3.45 px at @3x) and stays the same whatever the render size. The viewer applies the same rule at its own pixel density with 1.25 CSS px. Creases inside one part, found by the surface angle, are lighter than silhouettes and the lines where two parts meet. At 130 pt or less a crease needs 50° instead of 35° and is drawn at 60% of the width, so dense detail (the bread's cuts, the dice, the books' pages) does not clump.

Measured as the mean width (px converted to pt) of the run of ink-coloured pixels along every row and column of the stills:

| Display size | Before | After |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 120 pt (bakery, @2x / @3x) | 1.80 / 1.56 pt | 1.47 / 1.18 pt |
| 220 pt, now 352 pt (quiet street, @2x / @3x) | 2.48 / 2.06 pt | 1.80 / 1.42 pt |
| Getting started (shop, @2x / @3x) | 1.55 / 1.45 pt (240 pt) | 1.44 / 1.22 pt (312 pt) |

## Ground and video

Videos have no alpha. The scooter, shop, crossroads, quiet street, basket and box clips on a flat ground (the background or the card) stay MP4: the clips are 21 to 120 KB each, and the ground is known and flat there, so the baked colour matches the token (the encoder tunes it to within 1 per channel after H.264). Getting started stays WebP with alpha because its ground crossfades while the shopper drags, except the page 1 crossroads, whose entry would be 1.4 MB per scheme as WebP and is an MP4 that the screen swaps for the still while the pages move. Category cards have no clips. The whole set is 4.49 MiB (138 files), under the 5 MB budget.

## Viewer

Objects are listed on the left and the scene settings are on the right: replay entry, outlines, shadows, grid, auto-rotate, background, and light angle and height. Each panel has a button in its header that hides it, and [ and ] do the same; with the objects panel hidden, the page title floats at the top left and carries the button that brings it back. The docs sidebar collapses the same way, from the button at the left of the top bar or with ⌘\ (Ctrl+\). The viewer and the site remember these choices.

The viewport corners hold the controls. At the bottom left are the tools: Orbit (O), Pan (H, or hold Space) and Zoom (Z), plus Reset view (R); the dragged mouse uses the selected tool, a right-drag pans and the wheel zooms. At the bottom right, the camera (isometric, orthographic, perspective) sits next to the zoom control: −, the zoom level (back to 100%), + and Fit (Shift+1). Frames per second and frame time are at the top right. In the isometric view Orbit turns the object, so the camera keeps the true angle. On a phone the two panels fold into buttons at the top right.
