# Discover

Before choosing between findability and transaction, I set out to understand the high street from the outside in: what it is in 2026, what London shoppers say about it in public, and what the products already serving them get right and wrong. Hammersmith, where King Street meets the Broadway, is the worked example throughout.

## What shoppers say

The most frequent complaint is that you can't tell what a shop has, or whether it's open, without going there. When a need is urgent (eclipse glasses the day before, a fan in a heatwave, flowers on a Sunday evening), Londoners ask strangers on Reddit, phone around, or join WhatsApp groups that post stock counts with timestamps. They trust a neighbour's reply more than Google listings or shop websites.

After a failed trip, most say they buy on Amazon. Those who tried local, paid for parking and came home empty-handed say so in plain terms. The exception is when online can't arrive in time. Then a nearby shop that has the thing wins, at almost any price.

Loyalty to local shops has a limit. Shoppers will pay more at a small business "if it's reasonably convenient", and drop it after a wasted trip or hours that clash with work. The independent butcher becomes the place for anniversaries, and the supermarket gets the weekly shop.

Locals treat King Street as a street for errands and send visitors to Chiswick High Road instead. A product anchored here has to help people get things done before it can be a place to browse.

## High street data

Empty units are being let again (high street vacancy at 13.2%, the lowest since Covid), but September 2026 footfall fell 3% and online is 28.8% of retail sales. Jobs on central high streets moved from shops to cafés and services. Cash is down to 8% of all payments, yet still around a fifth of retail transactions. Policy is leaning towards small occupiers: lower business rates for retail, hospitality and leisure from April 2026, and a bigger relief threshold announced in the October 2026 Budget.

## Existing products

Across delivery apps, surplus-food apps and stock-search tools, the complaint users repeat most is being told something was available when it wasn't. One basket across several sellers now exists (Etsy, Amazon, and since October 2026 Shop app, and Google in the US only), but only with postal delivery. Nobody combines several shops on one street with collection at each shop or a single local courier run. Directories without live stock, like ShopAppy, haven't lasted.

## Findability or transaction

*Interpretation, to be tested in Define.*

The evidence supports both, and choosing between them is the first decision of the next phase.

- **For findability:** the strongest and most repeated pain is not knowing whether a nearby shop has the thing and is open. Small shops have no product catalogue to search, so whatever answers "is it there?" has to come from something the shop already does, such as confirming a hold or what passes through its till.
- **For transaction:** multi-shop checkout is arriving from big platforms, but without the local half: collecting from several shops, or one courier doing the rounds. Nobody has claimed that half yet.
- **How each constrains the other:** a shopper can only build a basket across shops if they first trust that the items are there. A promise of availability is only worth something if the shop holds the item once the shopper commits.

## Open questions for Define

- The shopper to design for. The [proto-personas](personas/index.md) put the strongest pain with Sam, the mission shopper, who needs a specific thing soon.
- How does a shop with no product codes say what it has, with almost no extra work?
- Is collection the default and delivery the option, given most people reach their high street on foot?
- What would the street's existing organisers, such as the Hammersmith BID, need to see to take part?

## Known gaps

X and TikTok were read, but X carries little shopping talk and TikTok comments couldn't be collected, so younger shoppers are seen mostly through creators' captions. Instagram and Nextdoor weren't collected, so older residents in neighbourhood groups are under-represented. Nobody was interviewed: every quote occurred naturally, and no one was asked about an app. Both gaps are listed on the [method](netnography/method.md) page.
