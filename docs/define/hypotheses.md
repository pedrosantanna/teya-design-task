# Hypotheses

The concept rests on nineteen hypotheses. Most come from an assumption or open question in the [Discover conclusion](../discover/conclusion.md); four come from my reasons for making the concierge an agent, five from how it makes money ([value and revenue](revenue.md)) and three from ratings on the shop page (decision 047). Each has a test. Fifteen have a go mark and a stop mark set before testing, and the result between them means iterate; H10, H11, H15 and H16 are exploratory and record a split or a count. Results are reported with their 90% Wilson interval, as the [measurement plan](../verify/measurement.md#go-iterate-or-stop) explains. They are ordered by risk: how badly the concept fails if the hypothesis is wrong, and how little evidence there is today.

```summary
Takeaways:
- The riskiest hypothesis is on the merchant side: shops answering an ask within minutes (H1). No evidence exists, and without it the concierge doesn't work.
- My main hypothesis about the product is that shoppers would rather ask one concierge than browse and phone shops themselves (H3), and that they accept a wait while it works (H4).
- Two cheap tests cover most of the risk: a four-week proof of concept with 10 shops around Blythe Road and 20 shoppers (about 60 requests, about 160 asks), and a clickable prototype with the same twenty shoppers.
Call:
- I added the four hypotheses about the agent (asking first, the wait, requests left ahead, channels) and set the order: the merchant side is tested first, by hand, before anything that depends on it is built.
- I moved the hand test to WhatsApp, with me as the concierge. It tests the shops, the wait and one of the channels at once.
- I set the go and stop marks before testing, so a result can't be read to fit. A prototype result records what shoppers say they would do; where it's cheap, the hand-run concierge checks the same thing with real requests (H2, H7), and behaviour wins when the two disagree.
- Later I added three hypotheses about ratings: that Snuffle's signals let an asked shop compete with a connected one (H17), that "% would go back" reads as easily as stars (H18), and that shops accept a public stock-accuracy figure (H19).
- I asked where the numbers came from, then sized the proof of concept from its funnel and set every mark against the liquidity model and a few outside anchors (decision 070). Where 10 shops or 20 pairs can only show a large effect, the mark is labelled direction only.
AI:
- An agent drafted the hypotheses from the assumptions and the concept, and proposed tests and pass marks. I added the four about the agent and set the order.
- An agent drafted H17 to H19 and their tests from decision 047; I chose the 5-second test for H18 and asking the same 10 shops for H19.
- One agent searched for benchmarks and another audited the plan's numbers; I set the new marks, and an agent rewrote the tables (decision 070).
Trade-offs:
- Small tests give direction, not proof. A go with twenty shoppers justifies the next step, not a launch.
- A concierge run by a person hides how well the agents would do the same job. That is a later test, once the shops pass.
```

A prototype test records stated choice: what a shopper says or clicks in a scenario. It can't show whether they would go, pay or collect. For H2 and H7 the hand-run concierge adds the behavioural check, with real needs and real purchases, and when the two disagree, behaviour wins.

## H1's marks

The answer rate's go mark, 60%, is the liquidity model's base input r ([product metrics](../verify/metrics.md#liquidity)). No published figure exists for UK independents answering on WhatsApp, so 60% is my estimate. It sits below the bar platforms set for a "responsive" business: Meta's "Very responsive" badge needed a 90% response rate and a 15-minute response time ([Facebook Help Center, archived 2020](https://webcf.waybackmachine.org/web/20200509150311/https://www.facebook.com/help/988319821208361)), and Thumbtack counts a pro's lead as answered within an hour ([Thumbtack community](https://community.thumbtack.com/discussion/comment/1583)). The stop mark, 45%, is where I judge the model breaks: at r = 45% and h = 40%, a request needs 9 likely shops for 80% fill, 6 per kind and 36 shops in a ring, more than I believe one ring's independents can supply. Shops are clusters, so the per-shop rates are reported too, and the go mark needs 8 of the 10 shops answering at least half their asks.

The fill rate's go mark, 50%, is what the model predicts for 10 shops at r 60% and h 40% (52%). Published fill-rate benchmarks range widely and none covers this kind of marketplace ([Lenny Rachitsky, "The most important marketplace metrics", 29 March 2022](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-most-important-marketplace-metrics)), so the mark comes from the model. h is measured apart, so a miss can be told apart: shops too slow, or shops without the item.

## What each test needs

These tests run inside the [proof of concept](next-step.md): the concierge agent reaches the shops, and the shopper app prototype is what shoppers use. The hand-run version below is the fallback if the agent isn't ready. The [measurement plan](../verify/measurement.md#proof-of-concept) explains how its size comes from the funnel.

- **Concierge by hand over WhatsApp (H1, H2 and H7 follow-ups, H4, H6, H10, H11, H12, H19).** A phone with WhatsApp and the same 10 shops within 15 minutes' walk of Blythe Road for four weeks, 5 on the provider's POS and 5 on other terminals, in categories that came up in the netnography (hardware, florist, bakery, butcher, chemist, stationery). Twenty shoppers with real needs, at least ten of them working shop hours, make about 3 requests each over the first three weeks. I play the concierge. No build. A walk around the 15-minute ring after 6:30pm.
- **Prototype test (H2, H3, H5, H7, H8, H10, H17, H18).** The clickable prototype from Deliver, with scenarios taken from the persona posts (eclipse glasses the day before, a fan in a heatwave, a weekly shop after work). First a usability round with 5 to 8 shoppers to find problems, reported as findings. Then the choice measures with 20 shoppers, 30 minutes each, who can be the twenty from the hand-run concierge.
- **Shopkeeper conversations in week four (H9, H13, H14, H15).** The same 10 shops, each with its demand summary and, where it applies, the provider's offer.

## If H1 fails

The concierge as designed can't work. The fallbacks are narrower: only shops whose tills share stock (instant answers, low coverage), or a slower request with a promise of an answer by a set time, which fits Jess better than Sam.

## If H3 or H4 fails

The agent stops being the main way in. Browsing shops and categories becomes the main screen, and the concierge stays as a tool for the requests browsing can't answer.

## If H19 fails

Stock accuracy stays private: each shop sees its own figure, and the shop page shows only the answer rate and reply time.
